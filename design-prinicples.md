# Design Principles

A collection of practical UI/UX design principles for creating clear, usable, and user-friendly interfaces.

---

## 1. Make a Strong First Impression

Visuals should look good and attractive within the first **50 milliseconds**.

The text should make an impression within **100 milliseconds**.

When people look at a design, they should consider:

* Can I trust it?
* Is it understandable?
* Does it look real?

### Five-Second Test

Show the design to someone for five seconds, then ask:

* What is this?
* What can you do here?
* Would you trust it?

---

## 2. Focus on Every Detail

Focus on every detail; don’t ignore mistakes.

---

## 3. Use Smart Defaults

Use smart defaults to save users time.

> **Example:** If the user is in Bangalore, prefill the location field with **Bangalore**.

---

## 4. Give Users Choices

Give users choices, such as different pricing plans.

---

## 5. Make Actions Clear

A screen should contain clear actions, buttons, icons, or borders that make it feel active.

Ask someone to look at the screen without giving them instructions. If they naturally know where to click, the design is working well.

---

## 6. Use Familiar Design Patterns

Good design connects with existing design patterns instead of making users guess what something is.

Use patterns that users already understand.

---

## 7. Reduce Unnecessary Mental Effort

* Show only four fields per page because people can focus on only about four pieces of information at a time.
* Ask one question per page.
* Use plain English.
* Provide a clearly guided sequence.
* Give users fewer things to remember.

---

## 8. Keep the UI Consistent

Keep the UI consistent with other familiar patterns.

---

## 9. Help Users Remember Your Design

* Provide search suggestions.
* Display recently opened or uploaded files.
* Show users what they did previously.
* Provide tooltips or suggestions for new actions.
* Preserve the user’s progress.
* Design for occasional users, not only daily users.

---

## 10. Design for the Peak and the Ending

People don’t remember the entire experience. They mainly remember:

### The Peak

The most emotionally intense moment.

> **Example:** The peak moment may occur just before submitting a bill. Show users a short summary of what they are about to upload.

### The Ending

After users complete a form, give them a small celebration or a clear success message.

The ending can leave either a positive or negative impression.

---

## 11. Fake Progress Gives Users Real Motivation

Instead of showing users an entirely incomplete process, show them that they have already made some progress.

| Instead of showing | Show              |
| ------------------ | ----------------- |
| ☐ Add name         | ☑ Account created |
| ☐ Email            | ☐ Name            |
| ☐ Date of birth    | ☐ Email           |
|                    | ☐ Date of birth   |

This gives users a sense that they have already started and encourages them to complete the remaining tasks.

* Show a progress bar to motivate users to move forward.
* When users see that they have completed **50%**, it encourages them to finish.

---

## 12. Group Related Elements Together

Users should easily understand which elements belong together.

### Examples

* Place the username and password fields close together so users understand that they are related.
* Keep a product’s name and price close together.
* Use different colours for primary and secondary buttons.
* Keep related items consistent in colour, spacing, and alignment.

---

## 13. Users Don’t Think in Steps

When designing an app, think about the **goal of the application**, not just the individual steps.

> **Example:** A user wants to see the progress of their health, not just input workout schedules.

---

## 14. Give Users Value as Early as Possible

Spotify lets people log in and start listening. After using it regularly and building trust, they may purchase a subscription.

It doesn’t make them complete long forms and wait before playing music.

### Good UI Example

After someone submits a claim form, show:

> **Your claim for 2K is being processed. You can expect it to be reviewed within seven days.**

This message gives users trust.

---

## 15. Listen to What Users Say, but Observe What They Do

Listen to what users say, but design based on what they actually do—especially when they are tired, busy, or distracted.

> **Example:** Someone may want to improve their fitness, download an app, and then never use it for various reasons.

Instead of building the complete app immediately:

1. Offer one small feature or a simplified version.
2. Test it.
3. Observe what happens.

---

## 16. Users Feel Something First, Then Explain It Logically

Users judge an application based on its first screen.

It is similar to a restaurant: when people see a clean restaurant, they assume the food will be good.

### Test the Emotional Response

Show your design to someone and ask:

> **How does this screen make you feel?**

* Safe
* Confident
* Respected
* Calm
* Confused
* Annoyed

Then ask:

> **At what moment did that feeling begin?**

Observe reactions such as:

* Pausing
* Frowning
* Sighing

### Desired Feelings

| Experience          | Desired Feeling         |
| ------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Payment screen      | Safe                    |
| Insurance claim     | Informed and reassured  |
| Error message       | Supported, not blamed   |
| AI assistant        | In control              |
| Onboarding          | Interested, not trapped |
| Confirmation screen | Confident and rewarded  |

---

## 17. Design With Patterns Users Already Understand

Design interfaces using patterns that users already understand.

> **Example:** Users may expect the **Download** button in the top-right corner.

If you change something in the application:

* Inform users about the change.
* Explain that an improved version is now available.

---

## 18. Design for Different Users

Different groups of people have different preferences:

* Some prefer minimal designs.
* Others prefer designs containing more information.
* Test your design with its target users and ask whether it works for them.

---

## 19. Introduce New Features Gradually

Some new products fail because users are already comfortable with the old way of doing things.

* Don’t introduce every new feature at once.
* Allow users to adopt and become familiar with one feature.
* Release the next feature only after they have formed the new habit.

---

## 20. Avoid Feature Overload

Too many features can overwhelm users.

* More features mean more decisions for users.
* A large percentage of an app’s features may remain unused.
* Test whether users genuinely need each feature.
* Remove unnecessary or unused features.

---

## 21. Respect the User’s Attention

Users are distracted by many things throughout the day.

Before adding a badge, tab, notification, or interruption, ask:

> **What was the person doing before this appeared? Is it worth interrupting them now?**

Avoid making alerts larger, brighter, or louder unless they are genuinely important.

---

## 22. Design for Failure and Recovery

People don’t quit because they are lazy. They may quit when an application repeatedly fails.

### Common Causes

1. Error messages that don’t explain what went wrong.
2. Forms that reject input without explaining how to fix it.
3. Broken flows or links that don’t work.

### Improve Recovery

1. Test every possible failure scenario.

2. For every error, ask:

   > **Can the user take the next step right now?**

3. Replace passive error messages with clear actions such as:

   * **Retry**
   * **Fix**
   * **Contact Support**
